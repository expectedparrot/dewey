# Detailed notes

## Design and findings

In a health-screening interview with a virtual human, participants were led to believe that the interviewer was controlled either by a computer or by a human. Those believing it was computer-controlled reported less fear of self-disclosure and less impression management, displayed sadness more intensely, and were rated by observers as more willing to disclose.

## Assessment

Strongly relevant mechanism evidence: perceived automation can reduce evaluation apprehension even when the visible interviewer is held constant. This is not a comparison of different interviewer implementations, and willingness/observer ratings should not be equated automatically with accuracy or validity of disclosed information. The framing manipulation also means the result concerns perceived agency, not merely objective automation.

Full text location: *Computers in Human Behavior* 37 (2014), pp. 94--100.
